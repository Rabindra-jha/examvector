001_001_Topic_Governance and Compliance in a Regulated Program

Q1. Case Study A: A national insurance carrier is modernizing its claims and underwriting systems while remaining under active regulatory audit. The program spans several states, each with its own filing rules. Core policy-engine work follows a predictive plan because of regulatory sign-off requirements, while the customer-facing claims portal is being built iteratively. A prime systems integrator coordinates several smaller vendors. Executives want visible progress every quarter but are wary of compliance findings, and state-level teams currently escalate issues inconsistently: some raise everything immediately, others sit on problems too long. Regulatory guidance on data retention is still evolving, and interpretations differ by state office. Legacy claims data feeding the new system adds technical risk as integration deepens. The program also receives frequent change requests from both regulators and internal business units, and sponsors keep asking for steady, demonstrable value to justify continued funding.

State teams are escalating issues inconsistently, and executives want predictable escalation without adding a heavy central approval layer. What should the project manager do?

A. Set up a single governance model that defines who decides what, clear escalation thresholds, and room for limited state-level variation.
B. Require every state team to escalate all decisions above a small dollar threshold to the program sponsor.
C. Leave each state's existing escalation habits in place and step in only when a specific conflict surfaces.
D. Tell state teams to keep doing what has worked for them and revisit the topic at the next steering committee.
Answer: A

Explanation:
Set up a single governance model that defines who decides what, clear escalation thresholds, and room for limited state-level variation.
A defined governance structure, with clear decision rights, escalation thresholds, and bounded room for local variation, fixes the actual design problem instead of reacting case by case.

Other options are incorrect
Requiring every state team to escalate all decisions above a small dollar threshold to the program sponsor creates a bottleneck and treats a structural gap as a control failure.
Leaving each state's existing escalation habits in place and stepping in only when a specific conflict surfaces only patches symptoms as they appear rather than fixing the inconsistency itself.
Telling state teams to keep doing what has worked for them and revisiting the topic later leaves the ambiguity in place and delays a fix that's already needed.

Q6. Case Study B: A retail bank is launching a new mobile-first banking product meant to replace three legacy apps at once. It's a strategic bet: leadership expects faster onboarding, lower support costs, and quicker response to fintech competitors. Stakeholder groups pull in different directions: the marketing team wants speed, operations wants stability, and branch staff care most about usability for less tech-savvy customers. Core account and ledger components follow a predictive plan for security and audit reasons, while customer-facing screens are built iteratively with regular user testing. Multiple workstreams depend on each other and move at different speeds. As execution continues, some stakeholders engage early and consistently while others raise concerns only late, scope keeps shifting in response to competitor moves, leadership has now tied the launch to a major banking conference which adds schedule pressure, some workstreams are ahead of plan while others lag due to dependencies and rework, and sponsors keep pushing for early partial releases to validate assumptions and show momentum.

Stakeholder engagement has been uneven, with some groups giving feedback early and consistently while others raise objections only near the end, and this is now causing rework and delay. What should the project manager do?

A. Keep the current engagement approach and hold stakeholders to the participation commitments they already agreed to.
B. Look at how the uneven engagement is actually affecting decisions, and adjust the engagement approach based on that.
C. Move key decisions to a smaller group of stakeholders who engage reliably, to reduce disruption.
D. Escalate every instance of late feedback to sponsors so there's accountability.
Answer: B

Explanation:
Look at how the uneven engagement is actually affecting decisions, and adjust the engagement approach based on that.
Understanding the actual impact of uneven engagement, and adjusting the approach based on that, treats this as an engagement-design problem rather than a compliance problem.

Other options are incorrect
Keeping the current engagement approach and holding stakeholders to commitments they already agreed to assumes the issue is discipline when it may be timing or relevance.
Moving key decisions to a smaller group of stakeholders who engage reliably risks excluding voices whose late objections may resurface anyway.
Escalating every instance of late feedback to sponsors doesn't fix why the lateness keeps happening and can erode trust if overused.

Q11. A logistics company is building a shared route-optimization platform with development split between Team North and Team South, working in different time zones. Team South hits a technical blocker that threatens to delay a component Team North is waiting on. Both teams are stuck waiting for direction. What should the project manager do?

A. Ask Team North to quietly adjust their own plan and absorb the likely delay from Team South.
B. Move the blocked work to a different team without telling Team South until the decision is final.
C. Tell the sponsor a delay is likely and wait for their guidance before doing anything else.
D. Bring both teams together in a joint session to work out options for keeping the shared task on track.
Answer: D

Explanation:
Bring both teams together in a joint session to work out options for keeping the shared task on track.
Bringing the interdependent teams together to jointly solve the blocker is the project manager actively leading collaboration rather than letting either team absorb the impact alone.

Other options are incorrect
Asking Team North to quietly adjust their own plan and absorb the likely delay avoids addressing the shared dependency.
Moving the blocked work to a different team without telling Team South damages trust and adds coordination risk.
Telling the sponsor a delay is likely and waiting for guidance skips the chance to lead a solution at the project level.

Answer: D

Explanation:
Identify exactly where the knowledge gaps are and set up training so everyone understands how to apply the chosen practices.
Pinpointing the specific gaps and building targeted training closes the inconsistency directly and builds capability across the whole team.

Other options are incorrect
Giving the agile-related work only to members who already have experience narrows capability-building and overloads a few people.
Asking experienced members to casually share tips without a structured approach is unlikely to produce a consistent, reliable understanding.
Dropping the new practices to avoid confusing anyone avoids the gap instead of closing it and forfeits the intended benefit.

Q53. A project manager built a plan around an agreed 16-month life cycle. A sponsor now asks, due to shifting business needs, for completion in 9 months. How should the project manager respond?

Run an impact evaluation with the project team before responding to the sponsor.

Q54. On an agile team working from a prioritized backlog, an R&D executive occasionally redirects work to more urgent items that were never in the backlog, frustrating the product owner. The team needs a way to handle this without disrupting priority management. What should the team do? (Choose 2)

A. Keep following the executive's instructions, but hold longer iteration reviews to justify the changes to the product owner afterward.
B. Stop accepting work prioritized directly by the executive instead of by the product owner.
C. Ask the executive to route any requests through the product owner for consideration in the backlog.
D. Ask the product owner to step back from prioritization and let the executive set the backlog directly.
E. Give the executive direct access to add items to the backlog.
Answer: B, C

Explanation:
Stop accepting work prioritized directly by the executive instead of by the product owner. Ask the executive to route any requests through the product owner for consideration in the backlog.
Declining to act on the executive's direct instructions, and redirecting those requests through the product owner, restores the normal engagement channel without cutting the executive out.

Other options are incorrect
Continuing to comply while explaining afterward keeps the problematic pattern in place.
Having the product owner step back from prioritization undermines product ownership rather than fixing the bypass.
Giving the executive direct backlog access formalizes the bypass instead of resolving it.
